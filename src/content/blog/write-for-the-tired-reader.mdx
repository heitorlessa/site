Picture the person reading this. They are not at a desk with a fresh coffee and
an hour to spare. They are on a phone, on a train, between two meetings. Maybe
English is their third language. They are *tired*.

If your writing works for that person, it works for everyone. So write for them.

## Short sentences are a kindness

A long sentence asks the reader to hold several ideas in working memory at once,
then resolve them at the end. That is fine when you are rested and fluent. It is
exhausting when you are neither.

Break it up. One idea per sentence. Let the full stops do the work that commas
were straining to do.

## Prefer the plain word

There is almost always a plainer word that means the same thing. *Use* instead
of *utilize*. *Help* instead of *facilitate*. *About* instead of *regarding*.
The plain word is faster to read and harder to misunderstand — and for a reader
translating in their head, "harder to misunderstand" is the whole game.

This is not dumbing down. Precision and plainness are the same direction.

## Front-load the point

Readers decide in the first line whether to keep going. So put the conclusion
first, then the reasoning. If they stop after one paragraph, they should still
leave with the main idea.

> Make the first sentence carry the weight. Everything after it is support.

## Leave room to breathe

Walls of text are not just ugly — they are a tax on attention. White space tells
the eye where one idea ends and the next begins. Short paragraphs, the
occasional list, a heading every few hundred words: these are not decoration,
they are navigation.

A few habits that compound:

- One idea per paragraph; when it shifts, break.
- Cut the throat-clearing — the first sentence is often the real opening.
- Read it aloud. If you run out of breath, the sentence is too long.
- If a word isn't doing work, delete it. Then check whether the sentence got
  better. It usually did.

## The test

Read your draft as the tired reader. On a phone. Quickly. The parts where you
stumble are the parts to fix — not because you can't follow them, but because
you *wrote* them and still stumbled. Your reader has no such advantage.

Respect their attention and they will give you more of it. That is the entire
contract.
