When you open an `https://` page, your browser and the server do a quick,
careful dance *before* any real data moves. It is called the **TLS handshake**.
It decides who the server is, agrees on how to encrypt the conversation, and
does both in a fraction of a second.

Most explanations either wave their hands ("it's encrypted, trust me") or drown
you in cryptography. Let's aim between the two: enough to build a correct mental
model, nothing you don't need.

## The four ideas

Before the messages, four ideas. Step through them — the handshake is just these
working together.

Hold those four in mind — **identity, agreement, a shared secret, then speed** —
and the message flow below stops being mysterious.

Notice the shape: the browser sends its key share in the *very first* message, so
by the time the server replies, both sides can already compute the shared secret.
That is why TLS 1.3 needs only **one round trip** — the older TLS 1.2 needed two.

## What this buys you

Three guarantees come out of those few messages:

- **Confidentiality** — only the two endpoints can read the data.
- **Integrity** — tampering in transit is detected and rejected.
- **Authentication** — you are talking to the real server, not a look-alike.

> The handshake is not the encryption. It is the careful agreement that *makes*
> the encryption trustworthy.

## Where it can still go wrong

The math is sound; the edges are human. An expired or misconfigured certificate
breaks identity. A server stuck on an old protocol weakens agreement. And the
whole trust model rests on certificate authorities behaving — which is why
browsers are quick to distrust ones that don't.

Next time you see the padlock, you'll know what it stands for: not magic, but a
short, deliberate negotiation — identity, agreement, a shared secret, then speed.
